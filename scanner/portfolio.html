A record and a set of checks, not advice. The program never places an order and does not tell you what to buy or sell.
It is run the way Berkshire describes running its own: a few businesses you understand, bought below what you think they are worth, judged by the earnings they produce for you and not by the stock price. Only companies that passed the ownership questions can be added.

Where it stands

Are you beating an index fund?

Every purchase you record is matched with the same dollars put into the S&P 500 fund on the same day. Dividends count on both sides. Shares you sold stay in the record at the price you got, so the result cannot be flattered by dropping losers.

Things to look at

Hold or sell: a review of each holding

Berkshire's letters give four reasons to sell: the business got worse, the original judgement was wrong, the price went far above any sensible value, or the money has a clearly better use. A price move alone is none of them, for winners or losers. Each holding is checked against the business facts saved when you wrote your case, and against your own value. The program raises the points. You decide, and you write down why.
